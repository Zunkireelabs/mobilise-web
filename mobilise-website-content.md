## Blog Post: The Death of Curiosity in Marketing

**URL:** https://mobilise.agency/blog/?post=death-of-curiosity-in-marketing
**Title:** The Death of Curiosity in Marketing | MOBILISE
**Published:** 18 August 2026
**Meta Description:** Why certainty, dashboards and faster answers can weaken curiosity—and how marketers can use data and AI to investigate more deeply.

I wonder if certainty has become one of the biggest enemies of curiosity in marketing.

We have never had this much information at our disposal, yet I'm not convinced it has made us proportionately better at understanding people. If anything, having an answer available for almost everything may have made us a little too comfortable with the answers we get.

A dashboard tells us conversions are up 18%. Good news, the campaign worked!

Except maybe the competition raised its prices, availability changed, sales did something differently, or the category simply had a good month. The 18% is still correct; what deserves more examination is why it happened.

Data is brilliant at fact-finding, but quite poor at judgement - which typically comes from experience, context, observation and knowing what else to question. It also comes from being willing to ask why three or four times, particularly when the first answer looks perfectly reasonable.

I think we've become less comfortable doing that.

Marketing has spent years becoming more measurable, attributable, optimised, and efficient, much of it for good reason. Performance marketing accelerated this because results could suddenly be observed and acted upon quickly. The danger is that the faster we can observe, report, and optimise something, the less incentive there is to spend time wondering whether we have understood it properly.

There is an organisational problem here too. Data rarely reaches a decision-maker untouched. An analyst interprets it, somebody then turns that into a report, another person extracts a conclusion, and eventually it lands in a management presentation. The underlying number may not have changed at all, while the qualification around what it means has likely disappeared along the way.

The thing is - curiosity doesn't fit particularly neatly into this system. It takes you outside the brief. It makes you look at unrelated categories, speak to people who weren't in the research plan, challenge perfectly good data, and sometimes spend hours investigating something that turns out to be useless. None of this looks terribly efficient on a timesheet or dashboard, but a lot of good marketing thinking has always happened this way.

Curiosity also needs a little space. Some of my better ideas arrive while driving, in the shower, on a hill with no signal, or simply when nothing is asking for my attention. We've increased the amount of information marketers process while steadily reducing the time available to sit with any of it. I doubt those two things are unrelated.

AI makes this especially interesting. I use it extensively and it has increased the breadth of what I can research enormously. But its value depends heavily on what I ask it to do.

I could have asked AI to write this post from the original thought and had something back in seconds. Instead, I used it to interrogate the thought first. We questioned the premise, argued about where data helps and where it doesn't, looked for contradictions, checked whether the idea had already been debated, and changed parts of the argument as a result. By not asking it to write for me, I ended up using it to improve both my argument and my understanding of the subject.

That, to me, is a far more interesting use of AI in marketing. Not simply helping us arrive at answers more quickly, but giving us the ability to investigate those answers much further than we could before.

We already have extraordinary tools for telling us what happened. I think the better marketers will be the ones who remain slightly suspicious of how quickly they think they know why.

## Blog Post: Beyond the Four-Day Week: Why Flexibility Matters

**URL:** https://mobilise.agency/blog/?post=beyond-four-day-week
**Title:** Why Flexibility Matters for Better B2B Work | MOBILISE
**Published:** 6 March 2025
**Meta Description:** Why flexibility, clear ownership and disciplined systems help enterprise B2B marketing teams think better and deliver stronger work.

Flexibility works best when it is supported by clarity. Strong teams need clean briefs, clear ownership, and enough focus time to do meaningful work without constant interruption.

In B2B marketing, this matters even more. Complex campaigns often involve multiple regions, stakeholders, products, and audiences. A flexible team can move faster only when the system around the work is disciplined.

### Flexibility needs a clear operating system

Location and working hours are only one part of flexibility. Teams also need a shared definition of what good work looks like, who owns each decision, and when feedback is expected. Without those agreements, flexibility can turn into uncertainty and duplicated effort.

### Protect focus without slowing collaboration

Good flexible teams distinguish between work that needs live discussion and work that benefits from uninterrupted concentration. Briefs, decision logs, and focused review windows reduce unnecessary meetings while keeping stakeholders informed.

The goal is not simply fewer days or more remote work. The goal is a healthier rhythm where people know what matters, when decisions happen, and how the work will be reviewed.

## Blog Post: Dark Social — The Blind Spot in Marketing Attribution

**URL:** https://mobilise.agency/blog/?post=dark-social-attribution
**Title:** Dark Social and B2B Marketing Attribution | MOBILISE
**Published:** 4 March 2025
**Meta Description:** Learn why private conversations create gaps in B2B marketing attribution and how teams can combine qualitative insight with measurable demand signals.

A prospect may discover an idea through a campaign, discuss it with colleagues in a private message, ask a trusted peer for advice, and return to the website directly. Standard analytics often records only that final direct visit. The conversations that created confidence remain invisible.

### Why B2B journeys are difficult to attribute

Enterprise decisions involve buying committees, long evaluation periods, internal documents and repeated conversations. Influence moves between public content and private channels such as email, messaging apps, communities and meetings. A single-source attribution model compresses this complex journey into one convenient but incomplete answer.

### Combine measurement with buyer evidence

Teams can improve their view by combining campaign data with self-reported attribution, sales-call themes, CRM notes and content-consumption patterns. Ask buyers how they heard about the brand, but also ask what gave them confidence to continue. The second answer is often more useful.

### Design content for private sharing

Clear research summaries, practical frameworks, concise case studies and useful sales material travel well between colleagues. Add consistent campaign naming and tagged links where appropriate, while accepting that some sharing will remain untracked.

The goal is not to force every interaction into a dashboard. It is to make better decisions using measurable signals alongside the qualitative evidence that explains why buyers act.

## Blog Post: AI Influencers — Transforming Brand Marketing

**URL:** https://mobilise.agency/blog/?post=ai-influencers-brand-marketing
**Title:** AI Influencers and Brand Marketing Strategy | MOBILISE
**Published:** 22 May 2024
**Meta Description:** Explore how AI-generated influencers affect brand trust, creative control and audience engagement—and the safeguards marketers should put in place.

AI-generated influencers can appear consistently across markets, formats and campaigns. Brands can shape their visual identity, tone and publishing rhythm without the production constraints associated with a traditional spokesperson. That flexibility makes the format attractive—but it also changes the nature of the audience relationship.

### Creative control is the opportunity

A synthetic character can be designed around a specific audience and adapted for different campaign contexts. The strongest use cases begin with a clear role for the character rather than novelty alone. It should add recognisable value to the story the brand wants to tell.

### Disclosure protects audience trust

People should be able to understand when a personality, scene or endorsement is artificially generated. Clear disclosure reduces confusion and gives audiences the context they need to judge the message. Hidden or ambiguous use can undermine trust far beyond a single campaign.

### Governance must come before scale

Before launch, define who owns the character, which claims it may make, how training material is sourced, and how content will be reviewed across regions. Include legal, brand and cultural review in the workflow, especially when the character represents sensitive topics or products.

AI influencers should be evaluated like any other brand channel: by relevance, credibility and measurable contribution. The technology may be new, but the standard remains familiar—use creativity to build a relationship worth keeping.
